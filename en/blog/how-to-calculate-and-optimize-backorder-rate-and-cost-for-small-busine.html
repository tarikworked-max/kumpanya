How to Calculate and Optimize Backorder Rate and Cost for Small Business

Managing stockouts is one of the most critical challenges for small business owners. When a customer wants to buy a product but it is out of stock, it leads to a backorder, lost revenue, and damaged customer trust. To protect your profit margins and maintain high customer satisfaction, you must track, calculate, and systematically reduce your backorder rate and associated costs.

In this practical guide, we will break down the exact formulas, Excel spreadsheet structures, and step-by-step methods to calculate backorder rates, measure the true financial impact, and optimize your inventory replenishment strategy.

Understanding Backorders vs. Stockouts

A stockout occurs when an item is completely unavailable to sell. A backorder occurs when a customer places an order for an out-of-stock item, and you fulfill it later once new inventory arrives. While backorders are better than completely lost sales because you retain the revenue, they incur hidden operational costs:

  • Expedited shipping fees to receive stock faster.
  • Administrative costs for managing delayed orders.
  • Customer service overhead for handling inquiries and complaints.
  • Risk of permanent customer churn.

To measure this accurately, let us set up a practical tracking model in Excel.

Setting Up Your Backorder Tracking Spreadsheet in Excel

Open a new Excel worksheet and set up the following column headers from row 1:

  • Column A: SKU ID (`SKU-1001`)
  • Column B: Product Name (`Ergonomic Desk Chair`)
  • Column C: Total Customer Orders (`Total_Orders`)
  • Column D: Orders Fulfilled on Time (`On_Time_Orders`)
  • Column E: Backordered Orders (`Backordered_Orders`)
  • Column F: Average Backorder Fulfillment Days (`Lead_Time_Days`)
  • Column G: Expedited Shipping Cost per Unit (`Expedited_Cost`)
  • Column H: Lost Margin per Backorder (`Lost_Margin`)

Practical Numerical Example

Assume you manage an online furniture store. For SKU-1001 during the month of October:

  • Total Customer Orders (`C2`): `500`
  • Orders Fulfilled on Time (`D2`): `450`
  • Backordered Orders (`E2`): `50`
  • Average Backorder Fulfillment Days (`F2`): `12`
  • Expedited Shipping Cost per Unit (`G2`): `15.00`
  • Lost Margin per Backorder (`H2`): `8.00` (due to discounts offered for the delay)

Step 1: Calculating the Backorder Rate

The Backorder Rate measures the percentage of customer orders that could not be fulfilled immediately from current inventory.

Formula:

Backorder Rate = (Backordered Orders / Total Customer Orders) × 100

In Excel, enter this formula into Cell `I2`:

```excel

=(E2/C2)*100

```

Calculation for our example:

`(50 / 500) * 100 = 10%`

A backorder rate above 5% typically signals a structural flaw in your reorder point calculation or supplier reliability.

Step 2: Calculating Total Backorder Financial Cost

The true cost of backorders includes both direct expenses (like expedited shipping) and indirect costs (like customer goodwill loss and delayed cash flow).

Formula:

Total Backorder Cost = (Backordered Orders × Expedited Cost) + (Backordered Orders × Lost Margin)

In Excel, enter this formula into Cell `J2`:

```excel

=(E2G2)+(E2H2)

```

Calculation for our example:

`=(50 15) + (50 8) = 750 + 400 = 1,150.00`

Your business lost1,150 on a single SKU in one month solely due to backorders.

Step 3: Optimizing Inventory and Reducing Backorders

To drive your backorder rate down toward zero, implement these three operational rules:

  1. Dynamic Reorder Point (ROP): Never wait until stock hits zero to reorder. Calculate your ROP using daily demand and lead time:

`ROP = (Average Daily Usage × Supplier Lead Time in Days) + Safety Stock`

  1. Buffer Stock Maintenance: Keep an extra 10% to 15% safety stock for fast-moving items during peak seasonal demand.
  2. Vendor Accountability: Track supplier delay rates closely. If a vendor consistently pushes lead times past the agreed window, renegotiate terms or diversify your supplier base.
