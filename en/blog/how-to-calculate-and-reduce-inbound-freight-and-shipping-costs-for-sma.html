How to Calculate and Reduce Inbound Freight and Shipping Costs for Small Business

Shipping costs are often the 'silent killer' of profit margins. If you aren't calculating your true Landed Cost, you are likely underpricing your products and losing money on every sale. This guide provides the Excel methodology to track, analyze, and optimize your inbound freight.

1. Mastering Landed Cost Calculations

Landed cost is the total price of a product once it has arrived at your door, including every hidden fee. Using only the invoice price leads to inaccurate margin projections.

Set up your Excel columns as follows:

  • Column A: SKU
  • Column B: Units
  • Column C: Product Cost
  • Column D: Shipping Charges
  • Column E: Customs/Duties
  • Column F: Insurance/Handling Fees
  • Column G: Total Landed Cost (=C2+D2+E2+F2)
  • Column H: Cost Per Unit (=G2/B2)

Why this matters: By isolating Column H, you see the actual floor price you must charge to maintain your target margin. If your Cost Per Unit increases by even 5% due to shipping, your net profit could drop by 15-20%.

2. Benchmarking Your Freight-to-Sales Ratio

Efficiency is measured by your Freight-to-Sales ratio. While industry averages for small retail/ecommerce businesses typically range between 5% and 8%, any ratio exceeding 10% is a major red flag indicating inefficient logistics.

In Excel, create a monthly monitor:

  • Column A: Month | Column B: Revenue | Column C: Total Freight | Column D: Ratio (=C2/B2)

Apply Conditional Formatting to Column D: If the value > 0.08, trigger a yellow alert. If > 0.10, trigger a red alert. This allows you to identify cost spikes before they impact your quarterly bottom line.

3. LTL vs. FTL: Strategic Shipping Decisions

Consolidating shipments is the most effective way to reduce costs. You must understand the break-even point between LTL (Less-Than-Truckload) and FTL (Full Truckload):

  • LTL: Best for shipments between 150 lbs and 15,000 lbs. It is cheaper for small batches but prone to damage due to frequent handling.
  • FTL: Best for large volume orders. While the upfront cost is higher, the cost-per-unit is significantly lower.

Actionable Tip: If your LTL shipping costs exceed 12% of total inventory value, simulate a larger, less frequent FTL order in your planning sheet to see if the reduction in freight-per-unit offsets the cost of capital for holding extra inventory.

4. Avoiding the 'Accessorial Fees' Trap

Many small businesses get hit by surprise carrier surcharges. Protect your margins by watching for these three common traps:

  1. Residential Delivery Fees: Carriers charge extra for delivering to non-commercial zones. If possible, ship to a commercial address or warehouse.
  2. Liftgate Fees: If your delivery location lacks a loading dock, the carrier charges for a truck equipped with a hydraulic lift. Always request a dock-to-dock delivery if available.
  3. Inside Delivery Fees: Never request the driver to move pallets inside your store; this is a premium service that can add 50-150 to a single invoice.
