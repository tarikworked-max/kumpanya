How to Audit Your Inventory for Hidden Losses

Inventory is often the largest expense for a small business. If you aren't auditing your stock regularly, you are likely losing money through shrinkage, obsolescence, or poor reordering practices. This guide provides a 3-step audit process to uncover these hidden losses.

1. The Physical vs. System Reconciliation

Many owners rely solely on their digital records. However, a 'phantom inventory'—items listed in your spreadsheet but missing from your shelves—is a silent profit killer.

  • The Step: Select a representative sample of your inventory (ideally 20% of your total SKU count, focusing on high-value items).
  • The Action: Perform a physical count and compare it against your system records. If your system shows 50 units but you count 45, you have a 10% shrinkage rate for that item.
  • The Projection: Instead of generalizing, apply this 10% loss rate specifically to the total value of the items in that category. If your category value is 10,000, your projected loss is1,000. This gives you a realistic, evidence-based view of your shrinkage rather than a blind estimate.

2. Identifying 'Dead Stock' and Carrying Costs

Dead stock doesn't just take up space; it consumes cash that could be reinvested into your business. To calculate your true carrying cost, you must include the following variables:

  • Storage Costs: (Square footage allocated to the item * cost per sq ft).
  • Insurance: Annual premiums allocated by item value.
  • Opportunity Cost: The interest rate you would earn if the capital tied up in this stock were invested elsewhere (use a standard 5-10% annual rate).
  • The Formula: Carrying Cost = (Storage + Insurance + Opportunity Cost) / Total Inventory Value.
  • The Insight: If your carrying cost is 20% and you have 5,000 worth of dead stock, you are losing1,000 per year. If an item has had zero movement over the last 90 days, it is time to trigger a 'liquidation' action (e.g., bundle sale or return to vendor).

3. The Reorder Point Audit

Are you over-ordering? If your 'Safety Stock' is too high, you are tying up cash that could be used for marketing or debt repayment.

  • The Calculation: Safety Stock = (Max Daily Usage - Average Daily Usage) * Lead Time.
  • The Decision Mechanism: After calculating your safety stock, look at your reorder frequency. If your resulting Safety Stock value is 0, your supply chain is perfectly aligned with demand. If it is consistently high, you are over-ordering. In this case, transition to a 'Just-in-Time' model to minimize holding costs.
